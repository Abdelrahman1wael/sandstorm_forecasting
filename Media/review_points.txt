INTRODUCTION
Sand and dust storms (SDS) are among the most important atmospheric hazards affecting arid and semi-arid regions of the world. They occur when strong winds mobilize loose surface sediments and transport mineral particles through the atmosphere, sometimes across very large geographic distances. 
In East Asia, China represents one of the major regions affected by SDS because of its extensive deserts, drylands, fragile ecosystems, and proximity to major dust-source areas. The Taklimakan Desert in northwestern China and the Gobi Desert region extending across northern China and Mongolia are particularly important sources of mineral dust. 
Dust generated in these regions can subsequently be transported across northern and eastern China and, during strong synoptic events, farther across East Asia. Recent observations have emphasized that Mongolia and northern China can make substantial contributions to dust concentrations over northern China during major spring dust episodes.
The importance of SDS in China extends beyond visibility reduction and temporary deterioration of air quality. Dust storms can affect human health, transportation, agriculture, ecosystems, energy production, and socioeconomic activities. Exposure to high concentrations of dust particles can increase respiratory and cardiovascular risks, while severe events can lead to substantial increases in mortality and hospital admissions. 
A large Chinese epidemiological investigation published in Nature Communications demonstrated significant associations between sand and dust storms and mortality from a broad spectrum of causes, emphasizing that SDS should be regarded not only as meteorological phenomena but also as important public-health hazards.
 Similarly, recent research in China has shown that dust-storm particles contain oxidative-potential components and persistent free radicals that may contribute to respiratory and circulatory health effects. These impacts highlight the importance of reliable forecasting systems capable of providing sufficient warning before the occurrence of hazardous dust conditions.

China is particularly suitable for advanced SDS forecasting research because of the strong spatial and temporal heterogeneity of dust activity. Dust occurrence varies considerably among the Taklimakan Desert, Gobi Desert, Inner Mongolia, the Hexi Corridor, and northern and eastern China. 
The meteorological conditions that trigger dust emission are also variable. Strong near-surface winds are an immediate mechanism for mobilizing surface sediments, but the availability of erodible material is influenced by soil moisture, vegetation, land degradation, precipitation, temperature, drought, and other land-surface characteristics. 
Consequently, SDS formation cannot be adequately described by a single meteorological variable or a simple linear relationship. Recent analysis of China's major dust-source regions using observations from 2000–2024 demonstrated substantial spatial and temporal variability and identified interactions among meteorological conditions and vegetation characteristics as important controls on dust activity.
The complexity of SDS formation becomes even more evident when different temporal scales are considered. Immediate dust emission can be triggered by strong winds, but the susceptibility of the land surface to wind erosion is strongly influenced by conditions that develop over days, months, or even seasons. 
For example, recent research in the Hexi Corridor used observations from 2005–2023 and historical records extending back to 1960 to investigate the effects of precipitation, temperature, wind speed, and drought on sandstorm occurrence. The study showed that temperature and precipitation during preceding periods were associated with subsequent dust intensity and that drought conditions over periods ranging from one to twelve months could influence different levels of sandstorm intensity. 
These findings are particularly relevant to medium- and long-term forecasting because they demonstrate that dust risk is influenced not only by the weather immediately preceding a storm but also by antecedent environmental conditions.

Traditionally, SDS forecasting has relied primarily on numerical weather prediction (NWP), atmospheric transport models, and chemical transport models. These physically based systems simulate important processes such as dust emission, atmospheric transport, diffusion, deposition, and interactions with meteorological fields. 
They remain essential components of operational dust forecasting. However, their performance is affected by uncertainties in initial atmospheric conditions, surface characteristics, dust-emission parameterizations, boundary-layer processes, and atmospheric transport. The chaotic nature of the atmosphere also means that errors in meteorological forecasts can increase with forecast lead time. 
A recent study of northern China emphasized that conventional numerical dust models may have considerable uncertainty because of atmospheric initial-condition errors and difficulties in representing complex physical processes.
These limitations have stimulated increasing interest in machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) for environmental prediction. Machine-learning algorithms can learn nonlinear relationships between large numbers of predictor variables and a target outcome without requiring the researcher to specify the mathematical form of every relationship. 
This characteristic is particularly valuable for SDS forecasting because dust occurrence results from interactions among wind, temperature, precipitation, soil moisture, vegetation, drought, land-surface conditions, and atmospheric circulation. 
Algorithms such as Random Forest (RF), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCN), and more recent graph-based deep-learning architectures can therefore provide alternative or complementary approaches to conventional numerical modeling.
The application of ML to SDS prediction in China has developed rapidly in recent years. A particularly important advance was the development of ML-based correction approaches that combine numerical dust-model outputs with observational and meteorological information. 
A 2025 study focusing on northern China developed a multi-model ensemble machine-learning system for forecasting surface dust concentration. The approach integrated outputs from several numerical models and used ML to correct their systematic errors. 
During 0–72-hour forecasts, the resulting model substantially improved the correlation and error statistics compared with individual numerical models and conventional ensemble approaches. This evidence demonstrates an important principle: ML does not necessarily have to replace physical atmospheric models; instead, it can learn and correct their systematic biases.
The use of multiple data sources is particularly promising for improving SDS prediction in China. Ground-based meteorological observations can provide wind speed, wind direction, temperature, relative humidity, precipitation, pressure, and visibility. Air-quality monitoring stations provide particulate-matter observations such as PM₁₀, which can be used as an indicator of dust loading. 
Satellite observations provide information on aerosol optical depth (AOD), vegetation, land-surface temperature, soil conditions, and spatial dust plumes. Reanalysis products provide consistent meteorological and land-surface variables across large geographic regions. Combining these sources can provide a much more complete representation of the environmental conditions associated with SDS than any individual dataset.
The increasing availability of long-term satellite and reanalysis datasets also creates an opportunity to develop medium- and long-term predictive models. Instead of predicting only whether a dust event will occur within the next several hours, ML models can be trained to identify environmental conditions that increase the probability of SDS occurrence several days, weeks, or months ahead. 
This distinction is fundamental because the predictors that are useful at different forecasting horizons are not necessarily the same. For short-term forecasting, wind speed, pressure gradients, atmospheric stability, and existing dust concentration may be dominant predictors. 
For medium-term forecasting, precipitation, soil moisture, vegetation condition, temperature, and drought indices may become increasingly important. For longer-term prediction, seasonal climate variability and persistent land-surface conditions may provide valuable information.
Recent research in northern China provides particularly strong evidence supporting this concept. Wang et al. (2026) integrated remote-sensing and reanalysis data covering 2005–2024 and used XGBoost together with SHAP-based explainable ML to examine both immediate and lagged drivers of SDS. 
Their results indicated that SDS frequency increased after 2015, while the proportion of high-intensity events decreased. Importantly, the study demonstrated that vegetation, temperature, and moisture-related variables could influence SDS risk with an approximately five-month lag. 
The resulting XGBoost model achieved an out-of-sample PR-AUC of 0.843 and a test AUC of 0.935, with annual-scale R² values exceeding 0.70. These findings strongly support the potential of ML to move beyond immediate weather forecasting toward annual and longer-range SDS risk prediction.
The importance of lagged environmental variables is consistent with the physical nature of dust generation. Vegetation can reduce wind erosion by protecting the soil surface, whereas drought and reduced soil moisture can increase the availability of loose sediments. Changes in temperature and evapotranspiration may further modify soil moisture and vegetation conditions. 
Thus, an environmental condition occurring several months before a dust season may alter the vulnerability of the surface to subsequent wind erosion. Conventional short-range forecasting approaches may not fully capture these delayed relationships, whereas ML algorithms can incorporate lagged predictors and nonlinear interactions into a single predictive framework.
Recent research has also demonstrated that machine learning can help distinguish the drivers of different categories of dust storms. A 2026 analysis of 64 years of Chinese dust-storm observations showed that total dust-storm frequency has decreased nationally, but severe and extreme dust storms have become relatively more important since the early 2000s. Interpretable ML indicated that ordinary dust storms were primarily influenced by synoptic-scale atmospheric dynamics, whereas severe and extreme events were more sensitive to land-surface deficits. 
The study further showed that extremely low evaporation and rapid soil-temperature warming were associated with increased severe and extreme dust-storm frequency. This distinction is important for forecasting because a model optimized only for overall event frequency may fail to identify the environmental precursors of rare but high-impact events.
Another major challenge is the highly imbalanced distribution of SDS observations. In many datasets, non-dust days greatly outnumber dust-storm days. Severe and extreme storms are even rarer. A conventional ML model trained on such data may achieve apparently high overall accuracy simply by correctly identifying the large number of non-event days, while failing to detect the events that are most important for early-warning systems. 
Consequently, forecasting studies should use performance measures such as sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1-score, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC), precision-recall AUC, Critical Success Index (CSI), bias, RMSE, and MAE according to the prediction objective. Attention to class imbalance, sampling strategy, threshold optimization, and event severity is therefore essential.
The forecasting horizon represents another major methodological challenge. Short-term SDS forecasting and medium- to long-term forecasting should not be considered equivalent tasks. In short-term prediction, observed or highly reliable meteorological conditions can provide strong information about an imminent event. 
As the lead time increases, however, the uncertainty of the meteorological predictors also increases. This can result in rapidly decreasing forecast skill if the ML model depends heavily on immediate weather variables. Medium- and long-term systems must therefore exploit slowly varying predictors, lagged environmental indicators, seasonal atmospheric patterns, and climate-related information in addition to conventional meteorological variables.
Recent developments in AI-based weather prediction provide an important opportunity to overcome some of these limitations. In 2026, Wang and Hua developed AI-DUST, a deep-learning system designed to forecast dust directly from AI-generated weather forecasts. The system combines stacked Graph Attention Networks with physical constraints and a physics-based emission scheme.
 In operational tests during the spring 2025 dust season over East Asia, the model produced forecasts up to 10 days ahead. Its 24–48-hour Threat Score was approximately 27% higher than that of a benchmark operational numerical model across 14 strong dust events, while the 10-day forecast maintained a Threat Score above 0.22. This represents an important development because it demonstrates that AI-based dust forecasting can potentially extend beyond traditional short-range forecasting.
The integration of physics with ML is especially important for SDS forecasting. Purely statistical models can identify correlations but may fail when environmental conditions differ substantially from those represented in the training dataset. 
Physics-guided ML provides a potential solution by constraining predictions according to known atmospheric processes. For dust forecasting, physical information can include surface emission thresholds, wind-driven erosion, dust transport direction, deposition processes, mass conservation, and the relationships between soil conditions and vegetation. 
The recent AI-DUST framework illustrates how physical constraints can be incorporated into deep-learning architectures rather than treating the forecasting problem as a purely statistical exercise.
Explainable artificial intelligence is another important component of a reliable SDS forecasting framework. High-performing ML models can contain thousands or millions of parameters, making their predictions difficult to interpret. 
For environmental forecasting, however, it is important to understand why a model predicts increased dust risk. Explainable methods such as SHAP can quantify the contribution of individual predictors and identify important thresholds or nonlinear relationships. 
The 2026 Northern China study demonstrated that SHAP analysis could distinguish the dominant predictors in hyper-arid, arid-transition, and semi-arid regions, with vegetation health, soil moisture, and evapotranspiration playing different roles depending on environmental conditions. Such interpretability can increase scientific confidence in ML predictions and help translate model outputs into physically meaningful early-warning indicators.
Climate change further increases the need for improved SDS forecasting. Changes in precipitation, temperature, drought frequency, vegetation cover, soil moisture, and atmospheric circulation may modify the spatial and temporal distribution of dust emissions. 
Recent climate-modeling work using WRF-Chem under SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 scenarios projected heterogeneous changes in dust emissions across China, including potential increases in western Inner Mongolia and decreases in parts of the southeastern Taklimakan Desert. Such spatially contrasting responses demonstrate that future SDS risk cannot be inferred simply from a single national trend. Forecasting models must be capable of adapting to changing relationships between climate, land-surface conditions, and dust occurrence.
In this context, medium- to long-term SDS forecasting in China represents an important unresolved research problem. Although substantial progress has been made in short-term numerical forecasting, ML-based bias correction, dust detection, and regional prediction, relatively fewer studies have systematically compared multiple ML algorithms for extended forecast horizons while simultaneously incorporating immediate meteorological drivers, lagged environmental variables, satellite-derived indicators, drought conditions, and numerical weather forecasts. 
Furthermore, the optimal algorithm may vary according to forecast horizon and target variable. Tree-based algorithms such as Random Forest and XGBoost may perform particularly well with heterogeneous environmental predictors, whereas LSTM, TCN, Transformer, or graph-based architectures may provide advantages for temporal and spatial dependencies.
Therefore, the application of machine learning algorithms to improve the accuracy of medium- to long-term sand and dust storm forecasting in China provides a timely and scientifically relevant research direction. A comprehensive framework can integrate meteorological observations, reanalysis products, satellite-derived environmental indicators, air-quality measurements, drought indices, vegetation indices, soil moisture, and lagged predictors.
 Multiple ML and deep-learning algorithms can then be developed and systematically compared across different forecast horizons. Ensemble learning and hybrid ML–numerical approaches may further improve robustness, while explainable-AI methods can identify the dominant predictors and critical environmental thresholds.
Such a framework has considerable practical significance. More accurate medium-term forecasts could provide meteorological agencies, public-health authorities, transportation operators, aviation services, agricultural sectors, and renewable-energy facilities with additional time to prepare for hazardous dust conditions. 
Long-term probabilistic forecasts could support seasonal environmental planning, drought management, ecosystem protection, and climate-risk assessment. By combining large-scale environmental datasets with nonlinear ML algorithms and physically informed constraints, future forecasting systems may shift SDS management from primarily reactive responses toward proactive and predictive risk management.
Ultimately, the objective is not simply to obtain a higher statistical accuracy but to develop a reliable, interpretable, computationally efficient, and transferable forecasting system that maintains useful predictive skill as the forecast horizon increases. 
The recent success of XGBoost-based annual prediction in Northern China and AI-driven 10-day dust forecasting over East Asia demonstrates that this objective is increasingly achievable. Continued development and rigorous validation of such approaches could substantially improve China's capacity to anticipate SDS events and reduce their environmental, health, and socioeconomic consequences.
